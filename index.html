How does a touch become a picture? The sensor's skin is a soft gel under a thin reflective membrane. An object pressed into it leaves its shape as a dent. [Step 2] Around the gel stand coloured lights, low and to the sides. Seen from below, through the gel, each slope of the dent catches the light it faces: one side turns red, another green, another blue. [Step 3] A camera under the gel takes that picture. Colour now stands for slope, and the slopes together are the shape. Beside the simulated picture is a real ball on a real sensor. [Step 4] It is yours to try. So how can one model read them all? SITR asks each sensor to introduce itself. A ball of known size is pressed at nine places across the pad. Since the ball's shape is known, each picture shows how this sensor paints known slopes, there. [Step 2] Then the corner of a cube, nine times, which shows how the gel behaves at an edge. With the empty background, these eighteen pictures are everything the model itself is told about a sensor. Above, they are simulated; below is a real set.

Simulated picture, real model. Heights drawn × 2.

The picture and the calibration set go into one model together: SITR, a transformer that was trained only on simulated sensors. What runs here is that model itself, with its released weights, in your browser, on our simulated picture. [Step 2] Out comes a map of slopes; added up, it is the shape of the touch. Below it is the true shape, which only the simulation knows. [Step 3] In this simulation, swap the sensor. The picture changes completely. The recovered shape stays.

One more sensor, and this one is ours. MoiréSkin sees through its skin: no side lights, no gel. A press shifts two line gratings against each other, and its camera sees the lines move. (Move over the membrane to slide the object, and scroll to press.) [Step 2] SITR reads shading, and here there is none. Given this picture and MoiréSkin’s own eighteen calibration presses, the same ball and cube corner, its answer holds almost none of the shape. It was made for sensors that shade, and has never seen one like this. [Step 3] MoiréSkin has a model of its own, made for this one sensor and trained on presses from this same simulation, on nothing real. It reads the same press. [Step 4] Air holds MoiréSkin’s membrane. (Scroll to change the air pressure.) More air wraps the membrane further round the object, and the model then reads more of the shape. These are the simulation’s pressures, not the real sensor’s.
